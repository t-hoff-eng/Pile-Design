Pile Design

Axial pile design to AS 2159-2009 ·

Methods

The calculator finds the shortest single pile, loaded axially in compression, that satisfies the AS 2159-2009 strength and serviceability limit states. Each equation used is listed below with what it represents. Symbols are defined in Inputs explained.

1. Design criteria

R*d,g = φg · Rd,ug ≥ E*d

Geotechnical strength limit state (AS 2159 Cl. 4.3.1). The design geotechnical strength R*d,g is the ultimate geotechnical strength Rd,ug reduced by the geotechnical strength reduction factor φg. It must be at least the design action effect E*d (the factored ULS load on the pile).

wSLS ≤ wallow

Serviceability limit state (AS 2159 Cl. 4.4). The predicted pile head settlement under the serviceability load must not exceed the allowable settlement set by the structure's tolerance.

2. Load combinations (AS/NZS 1170.0)

E*d = max(1.35G, 1.2G + 1.5Q)

Strength combinations for permanent (G) and imposed (Q) actions. Other combinations (wind, earthquake, etc.) are not generated. Enter the governing per-pile actions if they control.

PSLS = G + ψsQ

Short-term serviceability combination. ψs is the short-term factor (0.7 for most floor occupancies, AS/NZS 1170.0 Table 4.1). For long-term settlement, use ψl (typically 0.4) in the ψs box.

3. Geotechnical strength reduction factor (AS 2159 Cl. 4.3)

ARR = Σ(wi · IRRi) / Σwi

Average Risk Rating. Each of nine risk factors (site, design and installation) gets an Individual Risk Rating IRRi from 1 (very low risk) to 5 (very high risk). Ratings are weighted by wi from Table 4.3.2(A).

ARROverall riskφgb low redundancyφgb high redundancy
φg = φgb + (φtf − φgb) · K ≥ φgb

Effect of load testing. φgb is the basic factor from the ARR. Load testing raises it towards the intrinsic test factor φtf (default 0.90 static, 0.80 dynamic) by the testing benefit factor K:

K = 1.33p / (p + 3.3) ≤ 1 (static)    K = 1.13p / (p + 3.3) ≤ 1 (dynamic)

where p is the percentage of the piles that are tested. With no testing, K = 0 and φg = φgb.

4. Vertical effective stress

σ′v(z) = Σ γihi − γw · max(0, z − zw)

Total overburden from each layer's bulk unit weight, less hydrostatic pore pressure below the groundwater level zw (γw = 9.81 kN/m³). This controls frictional (sand) shaft and base resistance and the API α ratio.

5. Ultimate geotechnical strength

Rd,ug = Rs + Rb = Σ fs(z) · P · Δz + qb · Ab

Shaft resistance is the unit shaft resistance fs integrated over the pile perimeter P along the embedded shaft. It is evaluated in 0.05 m slices, starting below the head or the "ignore shaft friction above" depth, whichever is deeper. Base resistance is the unit base resistance qb at the toe multiplied by the base area Ab. Each unit value is capped at the layer's fs,max / qb,max where given.

5.1 Clay (undrained, total stress)

fs = α · su

Adhesion between the pile and clay as a fraction α of the undrained shear strength. Use a fixed α (typically 0.45–0.55 for bored piles) or the API RP2A relation:

α = 0.5ψ−0.5 (ψ ≤ 1);   α = 0.5ψ−0.25 (ψ > 1);   α ≤ 1;   ψ = su/σ′v
qb = Nc · su   (Nc = 9 for deep foundations)

Deep bearing failure in undrained clay (Skempton 1951).

5.2 Sand (drained, effective stress)

fs = K · σ′v · tan δ,   K = (K/K0) · (1 − sin φ′),   δ = (δ/φ′) · φ′

β-method. Friction on the shaft is the horizontal effective stress (K·σ′v) multiplied by the interface friction coefficient tan δ. K/K0 reflects installation disturbance and δ/φ′ the interface roughness (Kulhawy 1984). The defaults change with pile type.

qb = Nq · dq · σ′v
Nq = eπ tan φ′ · tan²(45° + φ′/2),   dq = 1 + 2 tan φ′ (1 − sin φ′)² · k

Bearing capacity factor Nq (Reissner/Prandtl) and Hansen's (1970) depth factor dq, with k = L/D for L/D ≤ 1 and k = arctan(L/D) in radians otherwise. Base resistance in sand does not keep increasing with depth in reality, so set a limiting qb,max.

5.3 Rock

fs = a · √qu   (fs, qu in MPa)

Socket shaft resistance from the uniaxial compressive strength of the intact rock (Rowe & Armitage 1987). a ≈ 0.45 for smooth or regular sockets and ≈ 0.6 for clean, roughened sockets.

qb = 4.83 · qu0.51   (MPa)

Empirical ultimate end bearing of drilled shafts on rock (Zhang & Einstein 1998). Base cleanliness strongly affects this value.

5.4 User-defined

Enter fs and qb directly, for example from load tests, CPT correlations or a geotechnical report.

5.5 Founding layer checks

If the toe's embedment in the founding layer is less than the specified minimum, base resistance uses the weaker of the founding layer and the overlying layer. A warning is given if a layer starts within 3D below the toe with a unit base resistance below 80% of the founding layer's.

6. Settlement – Randolph & Wroth (1978)

Linear-elastic closed-form solution for a compressible pile in a soil whose shear modulus may vary with depth. Shaft and base are treated separately and coupled through pile compressibility:

Pt / (wt GL r0) = [ 4η/((1−ν)ξ) + (2πρ/ζ)(tanh μL / μL)(L/r0) ] / [ 1 + (1/(πλ)) (4η/((1−ν)ξ)) (tanh μL / μL)(L/r0) ]
ξ = GL/Gb,   ρ = Gavg/GL,   λ = Ep/GL,   η = rb/r0 = 1
rm = {0.25 + ξ[2.5ρ(1 − ν) − 0.25]} · L,   ζ = ln(rm/r0),   μL = √(2/(ζλ)) · (L/r0)

G = E/(2(1+ν)) for each layer. GL is the shear modulus just above the toe, Gb below the toe, Gavg the average along the shaft and rm the radius beyond which shear strain is negligible. The result is the head stiffness Pt/wt, including elastic shortening of the pile.

7. Settlement – Fleming (1992) hyperbolic method

Shaft and base load–displacement responses are each represented by a hyperbola and then combined, giving a non-linear curve up to the ultimate load:

ws = Ms Ds Ps / (Us − Ps)    (shaft)
wb = 0.6 Ub Pb / (Db Eb (Ub − Pb))    (base)

Us and Ub are the ultimate (unfactored) shaft and base resistances, Ms the shaft flexibility factor and Eb the soil modulus below the base. For a rigid pile ws = wb and P = Ps + Pb, solved numerically. Elastic shortening is added:

we = 4P(L0 + KeL1)/(πDs²Ec)   for P ≤ Us
we = 4[P(L0 + L1) − L1Us(1 − Ke)]/(πDs²Ec)   for P > Us

L0 is the length with no shaft friction (the ignored zone), L1 the length transferring load by friction and Ke the effective-length factor (≈ 0.45–0.5).

8. Required length search

The toe is moved down from (head + minimum length) to the maximum toe depth in 0.05 m steps. At each step R*d,g and the serviceability settlement (Randolph & Wroth, Fleming, or the larger of the two) are calculated. The required length is the first toe depth that satisfies both criteria. The lengths needed for ULS alone and SLS alone are also reported, which shows which one governs.

Learning

Background for each concept used in the calculator. The diagrams are schematic.

How a pile carries load

An axially loaded pile transfers load to the ground in two ways:

  • Shaft (skin) resistance: friction or adhesion along the sides, Rs.
  • End bearing (base resistance): bearing pressure under the toe, Rb.

The load carried by the pile shaft decreases with depth as friction sheds load into the ground. Whatever remains reaches the toe. Long piles in soil are often mostly "friction piles", while short piles socketed onto strong rock or dense gravel are "end-bearing piles".

The two components mobilise at very different displacements, which is why the settlement calculation treats them separately (see Settlement below).

Limit state design and φg

AS 2159 uses limit state design. Loads are factored up (E*d) and strength is factored down (φg·Rd,ug). There is no single global factor of safety. The equivalent factor of safety is roughly (load factor)/φg, for example 1.3/0.52 ≈ 2.5.

φg reflects how confident you are in the ground model and the construction. Poor investigation, variable geology or little construction control give a high ARR and a low φg (down to 0.40). Thorough investigation, proven local experience and load testing raise it (up to about 0.9 with extensive static testing).

Redundancy: a single pile under a column (low redundancy) has nowhere to shed load if it underperforms. A large pile group or piled raft (high redundancy) can redistribute load, so a higher φgb is allowed.

Investing in investigation and testing often shortens piles enough to pay for itself.

Effective stress

Soil friction depends on the stress carried by the soil skeleton, the effective stress σ′ = σ − u, not on the total stress. Below the water table, pore water pressure u pushes the grains apart and reduces σ′.

Above the water table, σ′v increases at γ per metre. Below it, the rate drops to the submerged unit weight (γ − γw), about half. A rise in groundwater therefore reduces shaft and base resistance in sand.

Shaft resistance in clay – the α method

Clay under the relatively quick loading of a building is analysed undrained, using the undrained shear strength su. The pile–soil interface usually mobilises only part of su, giving fs = α·su.

  • Bored piles: softening of the clay at the borehole wall during drilling and concreting gives α ≈ 0.45 (Skempton's London Clay value) to 0.55.
  • Driven piles: α depends on how "overconsolidated" the clay is. API RP2A relates α to ψ = su/σ′v: soft, normally consolidated clays (low ψ) give α close to 1, and stiff, heavily overconsolidated clays (high ψ) give α ≈ 0.5 or less.

Many practitioners also cap fs in clay (for example at 100–150 kPa) unless load tests justify more.

Shaft resistance in sand – the β method

In sand, shaft resistance is frictional: fs = σ′h·tan δ = K·σ′v·tan δ.

  • K, the ratio of horizontal to vertical effective stress at the shaft. Before construction it is K0 ≈ 1 − sin φ′. Driving displaces and densifies sand (K/K0 ≈ 1–2), while boring relaxes it (K/K0 ≈ 0.7–1).
  • δ, the interface friction angle. Rough cast-in-place concrete develops the full soil friction (δ ≈ φ′), while smooth steel develops about 0.7φ′.

Because fs is proportional to σ′v, it increases linearly with depth. Field data show it levels off, so a limiting value (fs,max) is normally applied.

End bearing

At failure, a failure surface forms in the soil beneath and around the toe. Its extent depends on the soil strength.

  • Clay: qb = 9su. The factor 9 is the deep-foundation value of Nc.
  • Sand: qb = Nq·σ′v. Nq rises very steeply with φ′ (see the chart), so small errors in φ′ give large errors in qb. Bored piles in sand also need large movements (about 10% of D) to mobilise full end bearing, so conservative qb,max values are used.
  • Rock: qb relates to the rock's UCS. A clean base is essential, because debris left in the hole acts as a soft cushion.

Weaker layer below the toe: the failure zone extends a few diameters below the toe. If a soft layer lies within it, the pile can "punch" through, which is why the calculator checks 3D below the toe.

Rock sockets

Bored piles are often socketed into weathered or fresh rock. Shaft resistance in rock is controlled by the strength of the intact rock and the roughness of the socket wall. Asperities on the wall must shear or ride over each other for the pile to slip.

The Rowe & Armitage correlation fs = 0.45√qu (MPa) is a widely used lower-bound average for regular sockets. Smeared, smooth sockets (e.g. drilled with bentonite or in clay-rich shale) can be much weaker. In Sydney sandstone and shale, Pells et al. (1998) give allowable and ultimate values by rock class.

For defects, weathering seams and degradation on exposure, use the rock mass rather than intact UCS, or cap fs and qb.

Settlement and load mobilisation

Shaft friction is fully mobilised at small movements, typically 0.5–1% of the diameter (5–10 mm). End bearing needs much larger movements, about 5–10% of the diameter for bored piles. At working load, most of the load is therefore carried by the shaft and the pile is stiff. As load increases towards ultimate, the base takes over and settlement accelerates.

Fleming's method captures this with separate hyperbolic shaft and base curves (chart, right). Ms controls how quickly the shaft mobilises.

Randolph & Wroth model the soil as concentric cylinders shearing around the shaft plus a rigid punch at the base. The result is linear, so it is accurate at working loads but over-predicts stiffness as the pile approaches failure.

Soil modulus E is the most uncertain input. It is strain-level dependent, and values for pile settlement are usually between the very small strain (seismic) modulus and the large-strain triaxial modulus.

The Randolph & Wroth model

Around the shaft, shear stress decreases inversely with radius, τ(r) = τ0r0/r. This produces settlement that decays logarithmically with distance until it is negligible at the "magical radius" rm (≈ 2.5L(1 − ν)ρ in uniform soil). ζ = ln(rm/r0) is the resulting shaft flexibility term.

The base acts as a rigid circular punch on an elastic half-space (Boussinesq), wb = Pb(1 − ν)/(4rbGb).

Pile compressibility (λ = Ep/GL) enters through μL. Long, slender, compressible piles (μL > 2) cannot transfer load to the base efficiently, so lengthening them gives diminishing returns.

      Limitations

      This tool is a design aid for preliminary and detailed design of single, axially loaded piles. Results must be reviewed and verified by a qualified, experienced geotechnical engineer. The limitations below apply.

      Scope of the design

      • Compression only. Uplift (tension), lateral load, moment and combined loading are not checked.
      • Geotechnical design only. The structural strength of the pile (AS 2159 Section 5, AS 3600, AS 4100), including buckling of slender piles in soft ground and stresses during driving or handling, is not checked.
      • Single piles only. Group efficiency, block failure, group settlement and pile–soil–pile interaction are not considered. Group settlement can be several times the single-pile value.
      • Negative skin friction (downdrag) from consolidating soft clay, fill placement or groundwater lowering is not included. It can add load and settlement and reduce available shaft resistance.
      • Pile self-weight and buoyancy are ignored.
      • Durability (AS 2159 Section 6: exposure classification, corrosion allowance, cover) is not assessed.
      • Only the AS/NZS 1170.0 G + Q combinations are generated. Wind, earthquake, snow, liquid pressure, thermal and construction loads must be combined manually and entered as equivalent per-pile actions.
      • Cyclic, dynamic and seismic effects, including liquefaction and loss of shaft resistance in liquefiable layers, are not considered.
      • Reactive soil (AS 2870) uplift on pile shafts is only addressed by allowing shaft friction to be ignored above a depth.

      Strength methods

      • AS 2159 does not prescribe calculation methods. The static methods used here (α, β, Nc, Nq, Rowe & Armitage, Zhang & Einstein) are established empirical or semi-empirical correlations with considerable scatter. Local experience, rock-class tables (e.g. Pells et al. 1998) and load tests should take precedence.
      • The β-method shaft friction and Nq·σ′v base resistance increase without limit with depth unless limiting values fs,max and qb,max are set. Always set limits appropriate to the pile type and ground.
      • Nq uses the Reissner/Prandtl factor with the Hansen depth factor. Other published Nq values (e.g. Berezantzev) may be higher or lower; results are very sensitive to φ′.
      • Each layer is homogeneous: properties do not vary with depth within a layer. Split thick layers to model strength increasing with depth.
      • Clays are analysed undrained (total stress) only. Long-term drained capacity in clay is not checked.
      • Silts, gravels, cemented soils, carbonate sands, collapsible soils, organic soils and peat must be represented by the clay/sand/rock/user types. Engineering judgement is required, particularly for carbonate (calcareous) sands where shaft friction can be very low.
      • Rock correlations are based on intact UCS. Rock mass defects, weathering seams, smear, degradation on exposure and base cleanliness can greatly reduce capacity.
      • Rock socket correlations are intended for bored piles. They do not apply to driven piles on rock, where toe damage and driveability govern.
      • The founding-layer checks (minimum embedment, weaker layer within 3D) are simple screening checks, not a punching shear analysis.
      • Installation effects (bored pile base disturbance, softening, bentonite or polymer filter cakes, CFA over-flighting, driving set-up or relaxation) are represented only through the method parameters.

      Reduction factor φg

      • The risk ratings are the user's judgement and must be justified. The tabulated φgb values, φtf defaults and K expressions should be checked against the current edition and amendments of AS 2159.
      • The testing benefit assumes the tests are representative, conducted and interpreted to AS 2159 Section 8, and achieve the required capacity. Where testing is the basis of the design capacity (rather than verification), apply AS 2159 Cl. 4.3.3 / Section 8 directly.

      Settlement methods

      • Both methods give the settlement of a single isolated pile at the head, assuming short-term (immediate) response with drained moduli as entered. Consolidation and creep settlement are not computed.
      • Randolph & Wroth is linear elastic. It under-predicts settlement as loads approach the ultimate capacity.
      • Fleming's method depends on Ms and the base modulus. The rigid-pile solution plus elastic shortening is an approximation for long, compressible piles.
      • Soil modulus is strain-level dependent and the dominant source of uncertainty. Use values calibrated to load tests where possible.
      • Layered soil is simplified to average, toe and below-toe moduli (Randolph & Wroth) or the below-toe modulus (Fleming base).
      • Differential settlement between piles and group effects must be assessed separately.

      Numerical and software

      • Resistances are integrated in 0.05 m slices and the toe is searched in 0.05 m steps. Round the reported length up to a practical construction value.
      • The first toe depth that satisfies both criteria is reported. Where a weaker layer underlies a strong one, a deeper toe may be needed even though the shallower one passes. Review the capacity profile.
      • Data is stored only in this browser (local storage). Clearing browser data deletes projects. Export projects to JSON for backup and record-keeping.
      • The software has been checked against hand calculations for simple cases (see the automated tests) but has not been independently verified. The user is responsible for checking results.
